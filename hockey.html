The basics

The rules that matter: one game, three periods, and a handful of rules that decide almost everything you'll see.

Goal

Get the puck fully across the opponent's goal line. Simple as that — most of the tactics exist just to create one clean chance at this.

Overtime

Tied after 60 minutes? Sudden-death overtime, then a shootout if it's still level — first team to score wins immediately.

Penalty

Break a rule (trip, hook, hold...) and you sit 2 minutes in the penalty box — your team plays a player short. That's a power play for the other side.

Offside

Attacking players can't cross the blue line into the offensive zone before the puck does. Whistle, faceoff, reset.

Icing

Shooting the puck the length of the ice past the opponent's goal line without it being touched stops play — teams can't just dump it and change.

Words you'll hear on the bench

The five terms that come up in almost every game.

Faceoff

Two players battle for the puck as an official drops it — how play restarts after every stoppage.

Power play

One team has more skaters on the ice because the other is serving a penalty — the best scoring chance in the game.

Hat-trick

One player scores three goals in a single game. Fans traditionally throw their hats on the ice.

Board check

Legally checking an opponent into the boards (the wall) to separate them from the puck.

Shootout

One-on-one against the goalie, used to settle a game that's still tied after overtime.

The ice machine

Between periods the players leave and this drives out. A few minutes later the ice is new. Scroll to walk around it.

1 / 5

1949

Built by a rink owner

Frank Zamboni ran an ice rink in Paramount, California, and was tired of how long it took to clean. He spent years in his workshop on a machine that could do it in one pass; the first one that worked, the Model A, rolled out in 1949.

2 / 5

15 km/h

Flat out, a slow bike ride

A magazine once put one on a test track and clocked 9.7 mph, a little over 15 km/h. On the ice it goes slower still: the driver is laying a sheet of water, not racing the clock.

3 / 5

1.2 km

One clean-up, one long lap

From above you see the pattern: overlapping ovals from the boards inward. Added up, the machine covers about three quarters of a mile every time it cleans the ice, and it does that several times a game night.

4 / 5

26 kg

The work happens at the back

The sled it drags holds a steel blade weighing about 26 kg that shaves the top off the ice. Augers carry the shavings forward into the big tank, and a cloth behind the blade spreads warm water that freezes into the new surface.

5 / 5

15 min

It used to take a crew and an hour

Before the machine, three or four people scraped the rink, shovelled the snow, sprayed water and waited: more than an hour each time. The Model A did the same job, alone, in about fifteen minutes.
